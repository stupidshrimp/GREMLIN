Analysis

Perform an Analysis offers four analysis types, each run on one asset at a time. All four start from the dispositions people save, on the Disposition page or from a record's link in an analysis table, except that PM effectiveness picks its PMs by their record class. The first card covers what every analysis reads; the other four explain each analysis in depth.

{# ====================================================================== Common inputs ====================================================================== #} {% call std.doc_card( "analysis-common", "What every analysis reads", "Dispositions, the Include in Weibull Candidate flag, work order dates and downtime, the readiness summary and the failure mechanism Pareto.", eyebrow="Before any analysis") %}

Dispositions decide what counts

No analysis reads a work order's text to decide whether it was a failure. A person decides and saves a disposition, on the Disposition page or from a record's link in an analysis table: a category, plus a failure mode and mechanism for a work order, or a reset decision and target for a PM. Saving again replaces the current disposition. Earlier ones are kept as history, but no analysis reads them. The rules for each category follow Reliability Engineering's REL-WBL-DAT-002 Failure Definition and REL-WBL-PLN-003 Data Requirements, which the Disposition page's column guide quotes.

The Include in Weibull Candidate flag

  • It is not ticked for you. Choosing Included failure, or Included PM reset event with an Approved reset, leaves the box as it was, so tick it on every record the analyses should use; Check all Include in Weibull Candidate ticks a whole page. Only a blank cell in an Excel upload falls back to ticked for those two.
  • It is always cleared for Excluded non-failure and Excluded mixed or contaminating, and for PMs whose decision is Rejected reset or Context only.
  • Clearing it takes the record out of every analysis on this tab: Weibull, the Pareto, the trend, the downtime drivers, and the failure side of PM effectiveness, even when its category is Included failure.

An included failure, as every card below uses the term

A work order on the selected asset whose current disposition is Included failure, with Include in Weibull Candidate ticked. The Pareto, trend, downtime and PM cards also need it to have a failure mechanism.

Work order date and downtime

Every analysis but the Weibull fit places a record in time with the first date it has, in this order:

{% call std.equation("C1", "the work order date is the completed date if there is one, else the start date, else the created date") %} tw= { completed dateif it has one start dateelse, if it has one created dateotherwise {% endcall %}

And measures its downtime in hours, with nothing below zero:

{% call std.equation("C2", "downtime hours are the larger of zero and Limble's downtime in seconds over 3600, with a blank counted as zero") %} dw= max(0,sw3600) {% endcall %}
  • A blank date (empty or only spaces) is skipped and the next one is used. A date that is there but cannot be read is not skipped, so the record has no usable date.
  • The Weibull fit uses the completed date alone, with no falling back to the start or created date: a life restarts when the repair is finished, and a work order with no completed date has not restored anything yet (REL-WBL-DAT-001 makes Completed Date the Weibull chronology field). Such an event is listed in the fit's event processing table and left out of its lives; see Events, life intervals and censoring.
  • A record with no usable date still counts wherever a date is not needed: the readiness summary, the Pareto, the counts beside each choice in the analysis pickers, the trend's Most Frequent and Highest Downtime cards, and the downtime driver's summary cards, distribution, asset totals and top events. It cannot be placed in a month, so it is left off every monthly chart and out of the trend's Fastest Growing and Most Improved cards. PM effectiveness leaves it out altogether, because it has to place each record in time.
  • Blank downtime counts as 0 hours, and so does negative downtime.
  • Dates are read in UTC, the time zone GREMLIN stores them in, so months on this tab are UTC ones. A work order completed in the evening of the last day of a month, plant time, can land in the next month here. The Weibull fit's life hours are the exception: they split days at midnight on the plant's clock, because that is the clock the weekends they leave out are kept by.

Why this differs from availability

Analyses date a failure by when it was dealt with, so the completed date comes first. The Availability card charges downtime to the month a work order was opened, on the plant's clock. The same work order can therefore sit in different months on the Metrics page and here.

What it answers

Weibull analysis describes how long one failure mode or mechanism lets one asset run between failures. Its two parameters answer two questions. β, the shape, says whether failures come early in life, at random, or from wear-out. η, the scale, says how long a typical life is. Together they give the chance of failing by any age, the mean life, and the ages by which 10% and 50% are expected to fail.

Choosing the population

A Weibull fit only means something for one failure behavior, so the analysis is always run on a population: either a failure mode, which pools every mechanism under it, or a single failure mode and mechanism pair. A population is listed once it has at least one included failure, with how many failures and PM resets it has.

  • A fit needs at least five lives that end in a failure, the threshold REL-WBL-VAL-001 records for the validated pilot workbook. Fewer give a β that looks precise and is not, since maximum likelihood overstates β on small samples, and the interpretation summary would still turn it into a maintenance recommendation. A population with fewer than five included failures can never get there, so it is listed but can't be chosen.
  • Mechanism is the preferred grouping. A failure mode is the fallback for when the records cannot support one mechanism (REL-WBL-DAT-002 §7.1), so a report on a failure mode has to say why one of its mechanisms was not fitted instead.

Events, life intervals and censoring

The timeline is built from current dispositions on the asset with the flag ticked:

  • Failures: Included failures with the chosen failure mode, and the chosen mechanism when the population is a mechanism.
  • PM resets: Included PM reset events whose reset target is the chosen mode, and the chosen mechanism when the population is a mechanism.

Each event is dated by its completed date alone (not equation C1; see Work order date and downtime) and the events are put in date order. Every one of them, used or not, gets a row in the event processing table under the Weibull data table, with a note in REL-WBL-DAT-004 §11's words saying how it was used. An event is listed there but left out of the lives when:

  • it has no completed date: Excluded - missing completed date;
  • its completed date can't be read: Excluded - date parse issue;
  • it falls outside the analysis window: Excluded - before analysis start date or Excluded - after analysis cutoff.

Life is then measured from each event to the next:

  • An interval that ends in a failure is a complete life, with failure indicator δ=1: Valid completed life from prior same-population event, or Valid completed life from PM reset event when a PM reset started it.
  • An interval that ends in a PM reset is right-censored, δ=0: the asset was renewed before this failure mode had its chance to fail, so all that is known is that its life was at least that long (Censored interval ended by PM reset event).
  • The interval from the last event to the analysis cutoff is the current life. It is right-censored too, because the asset has not failed again yet: Censored interval to analysis cutoff date, or PM reset censor to analysis cutoff date when the last event was a PM reset.
  • The first event only starts the clock (Initial occurrence - no prior comparable start point available). Nothing is known about the life before it, so no interval ends there. GREMLIN has no installation or replacement dates to start a first life from.
  • An interval with no scheduled hours in it, such as two events on the same weekend, is left out; its closing event still starts the next life.
  • A life that ends within an hour, in calendar time, of the event before it is flagged Check for duplicate in the data table's Check column. Two work orders raised for one breakdown look exactly like that, and the near-zero life between them pulls β below 1, the reading that steers away from age-based PM. It is flagged, not removed: a genuine repeat failure is real information about repair quality (REL-WBL-DAT-004 §12).

The first failure only starts the clock; the lives ending in failures are complete (δ = 1), and the life ending in the PM reset and the current life are right-censored (δ = 0). These are the lives used in the worked example.") %} {% endcall %}

The analysis window

The lives are built between an optional start date and the analysis cutoff, and both are dates on the plant's calendar:

  • Start date: blank means all history. A date counts from that day's first minute, and the first event on or after it starts the clock.
  • Cutoff: a date runs to the end of that day, or to the moment of the run if that day is today. Blank means the last completed Limble import, as long as that is later than every event, and otherwise the moment of the run. Censoring at the moment of the run would credit the current life with hours the database has heard nothing about yet, and a failure in them would be missed.
  • Editors set both in the Select failure group dialog that Perform Analysis opens; a Pareto click uses neither. A run after a disposition saved from the data table keeps the dates of the run on screen.
  • The result states its window and where the cutoff came from: the last completed Limble import, the cutoff date entered for the run, or the time of the run.

Schedule-adjusted life hours

Life is counted in scheduled hours, not calendar hours, so an asset is not aged while it is not meant to run. Each interval is cut at every midnight on the plant's clock. A Saturday or Sunday adds nothing, and a weekday adds its elapsed hours scaled by the asset's scheduled hours per day:

{% call std.equation("W1", "a life in hours is the sum over the interval's weekday segments of the segment's elapsed hours times scheduled hours per day over 24") %} ti= ∑s∈weekdays of intervali Δs×h24 {% endcall %}
Δs
Elapsed hours of the interval that fall in day s, at most 24: 23 or 25 on the two days a year the clocks change, because each day's hours are measured between real instants.
h
Scheduled hours per day: 24 for the Salvagnini assets 3101 to 3107 and the Hedrich dispensers 3154, 3142, 3023 and 3253, and 20 for every other asset.
{% call std.figure("W2", "A life from Monday 08:00 to the next Monday 08:00 is 168 calendar hours. The 48 weekend hours are left out, and the 120 weekday hours count at 20/24, giving 100 life hours for an asset on the 20-hour schedule.") %} {% endcall %}

Days are split at midnight in the plant's time zone, the one the Availability card judges months by (America/Chicago unless it has been changed), so the weekend left out is the plant's own Saturday and Sunday rather than UTC's, which runs from Friday evening to Sunday evening on the plant's clock. If that zone can't be loaded, days are split at midnight UTC and the result carries a notice saying so. The dates themselves are still stored in UTC.

Life hours are scheduled hours, so a calendar week holds five scheduled days of h: 100 life hours on the 20-hour schedule and 120 on the 24-hour one. That is how the page and the report turn η, MTTF, B10 and B50 into calendar weeks.

The Weibull distribution

With shape β and scale η, the chance a life lasts beyond age t (its reliability), the chance it has ended by then, the density of failing at t, and the instantaneous failure rate of a survivor are:

{% call std.equation("W2", "reliability is e to the minus t over eta to the power beta") %} R(t)= exp[−(tη)β] {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W3", "unreliability, the cumulative distribution, is one minus reliability") %} F(t)=1−R(t) {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W4", "the probability density is beta over eta times t over eta to the power beta minus one times reliability") %} f(t)= βη (tη)β−1 R(t) {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W5", "the hazard rate is the density over reliability, which is beta over eta times t over eta to the power beta minus one") %} h(t)= f(t)R(t)= βη (tη)β−1 {% endcall %}

At t=η, whatever β is, F(η)=1−e−1≈0.632: η is the age by which 63.2% of the population is expected to have failed. β decides how the hazard rate moves with age.

{% call std.figure("W3", "Hazard rate against age, in multiples of η, for three values of β. Below 1 the risk of failing falls with age, at 1 it stays level, and above 1 it climbs: the three patterns the interpretation rules below are built on.") %} {% endcall %}

Fitting β and η by maximum likelihood

The fit chooses the β and η that make the observed lives most likely. A complete life contributes the density of failing at that age; a censored life contributes the chance of surviving at least that long. In logarithms, with n lives of which r are complete:

{% call std.equation("W6", "the log-likelihood is the sum over complete lives of log beta minus beta log eta plus beta minus one times log t, minus the sum over all lives of t over eta to the power beta") %} ℓ(β,η)= ∑i:δi=1 [ln⁡β−βln⁡η+(β−1)ln⁡ti] − ∑i=1n (tiη)β {% endcall %}

Setting the derivative with respect to η to zero gives η in terms of β:

{% call std.equation("W7", "eta is the r-th part of the sum of t to the power beta, all to the power one over beta") %} η^= (∑i=1ntiβ^r)1/β^ {% endcall %}

Substituting it back leaves one equation in β alone, which the fitted β makes zero:

{% call std.equation("W8", "one over beta, plus the mean log of the complete lives, minus the t to the beta weighted mean log of all lives, equals zero") %} 1β+ 1r∑i:δi=1ln⁡ti − ∑i=1ntiβln⁡ti ∑i=1ntiβ =0 {% endcall %}
  • How it is solved: GREMLIN checks 400 evenly spaced values of β from 0.1 to 20 for a change of sign in equation W8, then halves the bracketing interval 80 times. η then follows from equation W7.
  • If there is no root between 0.1 and 20, the fit stops and says it did not converge. There is then no maximum-likelihood β to report, and REL-WBL-MTH-001 §5.5 makes β and η maximum-likelihood estimates, so no estimate of another kind is put in its place. It happens when the lives are all but identical, often because of duplicate work orders.
  • The fit needs at least five complete lives, lives that end in a failure. With fewer, or with no root, the run stops and says why, and any result saved for the group before is removed, because the data no longer supports it.

Confidence intervals

The approximate 95% intervals come from how sharply the log-likelihood falls away from its peak. GREMLIN takes its second derivatives numerically with respect to ln⁡β and ln⁡η, inverts that information matrix for the variances, and turns them back, so both ends of each interval are always positive:

{% call std.equation("W9", "the interval for beta is beta times e to the minus or plus 1.96 standard errors of log beta, and the same for eta, with the squared standard errors read off the inverse of the observed information") %} βL,U=β^exp(∓1.96SE(ln⁡β^)) ηL,U=η^exp(∓1.96SE(ln⁡η^)) SE2(ln⁡β^)=[ℐ−1]11 SE2(ln⁡η^)=[ℐ−1]22 {% endcall %}
ℐ
The observed information: the negative of the matrix of second derivatives of equation W6 with respect to ln⁡β and ln⁡η, taken at the fitted values. GREMLIN estimates it by finite differences.
1.96
The two-sided 95% point of the standard normal distribution.

When the curvature cannot be inverted the intervals are reported as Not available. They are approximate either way: with few failures they are wide, and intervals of this kind also tend to come out narrower than they should when failures are few, so read a narrow one on a small sample with care.

MTTF, B10, B50 and fit statistics

{% call std.equation("W10", "mean time to failure is eta times the gamma function of one plus one over beta") %} MTTF=ηΓ(1+1β) {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W11", "B10 life is eta times minus log 0.9 to the power one over beta, and B50 life is eta times log 2 to the power one over beta") %} B10=η(−ln⁡0.90)1/β B50=η(ln⁡2)1/β {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W12", "AIC is four minus twice the maximum log-likelihood and BIC is two log n minus twice the maximum log-likelihood") %} AIC=4−2ℓ^ BIC=2ln⁡n−2ℓ^ {% endcall %}
  • MTTF is the mean life. It is shown with the result and in the report.
  • B10 and B50 are the ages by which 10% and 50% are expected to fail; B50 is the median life. Both are shown under the fitted values and in the report.
  • η, MTTF, B10 and B50 are each shown with the calendar weeks that many life hours take on the result's schedule (see Schedule-adjusted life hours).
  • AIC and BIC score the fit against its two parameters; lower is better when comparing fits of the same lives. They are stored with every saved result but not shown.

Reliability at an age: under the charts, enter an age in life hours, such as a PM interval being considered, and the page gives R(t) and F(t) there, from equations W2 and W3: the share expected to survive to that age and to fail by it. It uses the fitted values, or the values an editor is trying, and says which. A report generated with an age entered states it at the fitted values (REL-WBL-MTH-001 §5.7 and §10).

The four charts

The points for complete lives are not the fit: they are what the lives themselves say, estimated without assuming any distribution by the Kaplan-Meier method. (The red points for lives ended by a PM reset only mark their ages, on the fitted curve.) At each age where a complete life ends:

{% call std.equation("W13", "the Kaplan-Meier reliability at a failure age is the product over failure ages up to it of one minus failures over the number at risk") %} R^(tj)= ∏ti≤tj (1−dini) {% endcall %}
di
Complete lives ending at age ti.
ni
Lives still at risk just before ti. A censored life counts as at risk up to and including its own age, then leaves.

The probability plot straightens a Weibull curve. Taking logarithms of equation W2 twice turns it into a line whose slope is β and which crosses zero at t=η:

{% call std.equation("W14", "the probability plot puts log of minus log reliability against log t, where a Weibull is the line beta log t minus beta log eta") %} x=ln⁡t, y=ln⁡[−ln⁡R(t)] ⇒ y=βln⁡t−βln⁡η {% endcall %}
  • Weibull Probability Plot: the Kaplan-Meier points for the complete lives, the fitted line (green) and, where the intervals exist, the lines at the lower and upper ends of both intervals (yellow). Points that lie close to the green line support the Weibull model. A point whose estimate is 0 or 1 cannot be drawn on this scale.
  • CDF: the fitted F(t) with the Kaplan-Meier points for complete lives (white, or dark in the dark theme) and the lives that ended in a PM reset (red), placed on the fitted curve at their age.
  • Probability Density (PDF) and Hazard Rate: equations W4 and W5 as curves alone, with no points or interval curves, drawn at the fitted values or at the values an editor is trying.
  • The red vertical line in every pane is the current life: the scheduled hours from the most recent event to the run's analysis cutoff.
  • Hovering over a point shows its work order; clicking it jumps to its row in the Weibull data table, and clicking a row rings its point on the chart.
  • The Weibull data table under the charts lists each life with its type, its life hours, the raw elapsed hours between its two events and the weekend and non-run hours taken out of them (REL-WBL-DAT-004 §5), its note, and the duplicate check. The event processing table under it, closed until opened, lists every event behind those lives, including the ones left out and why.

How straight the points lie is measured by the probability plot's R²: the squared correlation of the plotted points, which is also the R² of the least-squares line through them (REL-WBL-REQ-001 VV-070). It is 1 when they sit exactly on a line. It checks the model, not the fit: β and η come from equation W8, not from a line through these points, so adjusting them leaves R² where it is.

{% call std.equation("W15", "R squared is the squared sum of products of the plotted points' deviations from their means, over the product of the sums of their squared deviations") %} R2= [∑j(xj−x¯)(yj−y¯)]2 ∑j(xj−x¯)2∑j(yj−y¯)2 {% endcall %}
xj,yj
The points on the probability plot, equation W14 at each age where a complete life ends. A point the plot cannot draw, with an estimate of 0, is left out here too.
x¯,y¯
Their averages. With fewer than three points R² is not shown, because two points always lie on a line.

GREMLIN sets no pass mark for R², so read it with the plot. A low value, a bend or two different slopes usually means the population mixes mechanisms, a life is missing its real start point, or a work order is a duplicate: review the population before acting on β (VV-074). R² is shown with the results, in the interpretation summary and the report, and beside each fit in the Highest-beta list.

{% call std.figure("W4", "The worked example on probability paper. The fitted line's slope is β = 2.74 and it crosses the 63.2% line at η = 847 hours. The five Kaplan-Meier points run roughly parallel to it, all a little above it and the first most of all: with five failures each Kaplan-Meier step is a big one, and the sample is small, which is also why the confidence intervals are wide. Among themselves the points are very nearly straight: their R², equation W15, is 0.990.") %} {% endcall %}

Reading the result

The Results Interpretation Summary turns β, η, MTTF, the two intervals and the probability plot's R² into recommendations with these rules:

Weibull interpretation rules
ResultReadingSuggested direction
β below 0.9Early-life or defect-driven failuresLook at installation, setup, commissioning and repair quality, and latent defects. Age-based replacement is not supported.
β from 0.9 to 1.1Random failuresImprove detection with inspections or condition checks, and plan spares. A fixed replacement age is weak justification.
β above 1.1Wear-outEvaluate an age-based PM or planned replacement, set with η, downtime impact and cost.
β interval crosses 1.0Pattern still uncertainCheck whether the population mixes mechanisms or needs more failures before choosing a strategy.
β interval no wider than 0.3, not crossing 1.0StableMore confidence in the direction above.
β interval wider than 0.3, not crossing 1.0Treat with cautionCheck whether the population mixes mechanisms, the data is weak, or there are still too few failures before choosing a strategy.
η interval width at most 40% of ηStable ηUsable for planning comparisons and timing discussions.
η interval wider than thatDirectional onlyTighten the population or collect more data before treating η as a decision point.
Probability plot R², equation W15How straight the plotted points lie; no fixed pass markRead it with the plot. A low value, a bend or two slopes calls for a review of the population before acting on β.

MTTF is for budgeting, manpower and spares, not for timing a PM on its own: in a population that is not wearing out, replacing at the average life adds work without removing failures.

  • B10 and B50, equation W11372.5 h and 741.1 h
  • Reliability at 400 h, equation W2exp(−(400 / 847.2)2.739) = 0.880, so F(400) = 0.120
  • Log-likelihood, AIC, BIC−36.69; 77.38; 77.28

Reading it: the five points lie close to a line (R² = 0.990), so the plot gives no sign of a mixed population. β = 2.74 is above 1.1, which points to wear-out, and with five failures its interval, 1.35 to 5.56, stays above 1.0. It is still far wider than 0.3, though, so the pattern is to be treated with caution, and η's interval is 65% of η, too wide to set a PM interval from. A PM every 400 hours would leave about 12% failing before it. The honest conclusion is "wearing out, most likely; collect more failures before fixing a PM interval".

Cut off 1,050 hours after its third complete life, as this example used to be, it would not be fitted at all: three lives ending in a failure is under GREMLIN's minimum of five, and the β interval of that fit, 0.84 to 5.35, crossed 1.0.

Adjusting, saving and reporting

  • Each failure group keeps only its latest run. A run saves its dataset, lives, Kaplan-Meier points, curve and result, stamped with its analysis window, and viewers see that saved result. Running the group again picks up every disposition saved since and replaces the previous run, along with any adjusted parameters saved against it, so earlier runs are not kept. A run the data can no longer support, with fewer than five failure lives or no likelihood root, removes the group's saved result too. A failure mode and each mechanism under it are separate groups: running one leaves the others' saved results as they were.
  • Every run is stamped with the method it was built by (life-data-v2 today) and the GREMLIN code it ran on. A result saved under an earlier method shows a notice saying what has changed since; it stays in the Highest-beta list, marked, but can't be reported until it is run again. One resting on fewer than five failure lives is not ranked or reported either.
  • Editors can try other values of β and η (the inputs step by 0.2 and by 100 hours) and the charts redraw as they type. Save Adjusted Parameters stores the pair with a reason beside the fitted result, never replacing it. A saved pair lasts until the group is run again, and the page does not show it again: the inputs always open on the fitted values.
  • Generate Weibull Report, for editors, writes a Word report numbered REL-WBL-RPT, the asset number and a running number. It carries what REL-WBL-MTH-001 §10 asks of an analysis package, at the fitted values: the population and its grouping level; the life basis, schedule and time zone, labelled as an exposure proxy; the analysis window and where its cutoff came from; the lives, split into complete, censored at a PM reset and current; β, η, MTTF, B10, B50, their intervals and the reliability at the age entered on the page; the charts; the interpretation summary; the limitations and assumptions; every life it rests on, with its notes; and the method and code version. It opens with REL-WBL-VAL-001's caveat that GREMLIN's Weibull automation is not yet validated, so the report is for reliability engineering review, not a stand-alone maintenance directive. A failure-mode report also asks for, states and keeps with the population the reason a mechanism could not be fitted instead (REL-WBL-PLN-003 §8). The report is downloaded rather than stored: GREMLIN logs its number, so that no number is used twice, but keeps no copy. Keep the download if a run's results need a record, because running the group again replaces them.
  • Clicking a Task ID in the Weibull data table opens that work order's disposition for an editor; saving it runs the analysis again, which replaces the saved run as above. Viewers see the Task ID as plain text.

Limitations

  • Few failures give wide intervals. Read β and η as directions until the intervals narrow.
  • A failure mode that pools different mechanisms mixes different behaviors and blurs β. Fit the mechanism when there are enough failures.
  • Life is scheduled time, not run time. It uses the 24 or 20 hour weekday schedule, not meter hours, and ignores holidays, shutdowns and Saturday shifts.
  • No installation or replacement dates. The first event in the window only starts the clock, so the life before it is never counted.
  • Only two parameters. There is no failure-free period (three-parameter Weibull) and no comparison against other distributions.
  • Not yet validated. REL-WBL-VAL-001 validates the manual pilot workbook, not GREMLIN, and does not yet approve automated use (§7). Until GREMLIN's own results have been checked against the pilot baseline, use them for reliability engineering review rather than as stand-alone maintenance decisions.

What it counts

Every included failure on the asset that has a failure mechanism: the same records as the Pareto. Each one is put in the month of its work order date (equation C1, in UTC). The months run without gaps from the earliest to the latest month in which the asset has any included failure, and a month with none counts as 0, so the line never skips a quiet month.

Pick a mechanism from the Pareto or the analysis controls to plot that mechanism alone, or pick a failure mode to add up every mechanism under it:

{% call std.equation("T1", "the count for a month is the sum over the selected mechanisms of their failures in that month") %} c(m)= ∑k∈K nk(m) {% endcall %}
K
The selected mechanism, or every mechanism under the selected failure mode.
nk(m)
Included failures of mechanism k dated in month m.

A record without any usable date still counts toward a mechanism's total but cannot be placed in a month, so it is not on the line or in the tables below it.

The four summary cards

The cards look across every mechanism on the asset, not only the one selected:

  • Most Frequent: the mechanism with the most included failures; ties go to the one with more downtime.
  • Highest Downtime: the mechanism with the most downtime hours; ties go to the one with more failures.
  • Fastest Growing and Most Improved: the mechanisms with the largest rise and the largest fall between the previous three months and the last three:
{% call std.equation("T2", "growth is the failures in the last three months minus the failures in the three months before them") %} Gk= ∑m∈last 3 monthsnk(m) − ∑m∈previous 3 monthsnk(m) {% endcall %}
  • The last three months are the last three on the month axis, whether or not they had failures.
  • Growth needs at least six months on the axis. With fewer, both cards say Insufficient Data.
  • Fastest Growing only considers mechanisms that rose, and Most Improved only those that fell. When none moved that way the card says so ("No mechanism increased", "No mechanism decreased").
  • The cards show the change as "+N occ. vs. prior 3 mo" or "−N occ. vs. prior 3 mo".

Chart, tables and date range

  • Failure Mode Trend plots c(m) for each month.
  • Failure Mode Trend Detail lists the same monthly counts with a total.
  • Work Orders in Trend lists the work orders behind the counts. Clicking a month in the detail table, or a point on the chart, shows only that month's work orders.
  • From and To narrow the chart and both tables to a range of months. They do not change the summary cards, which always use the whole history.

Reading it: a rising line on a mechanism that already tops the Pareto is the first place to look. Six months is a short window, so a single busy month can swing the growth cards; check the Work Orders in Trend list before acting on one.

What it reads

The included failures with a mechanism on the asset, for the selected mechanism, or for every mechanism under the selected failure mode. Downtime is equation C2, so blank and negative values count as 0 hours, and every included failure counts toward the summary cards and the distribution whether or not it has a date.

The five summary cards

{% call std.equation("D1", "total downtime is the sum of each failure's downtime, and the average is the total over the number of work orders") %} D=∑i=1ndi d‾=Dn {% endcall %}
  • Total Downtime is D and Work Order Count is n.
  • Average Downtime is d‾.
  • Median Downtime is the middle value once sorted, or the mean of the two middle values when there is an even number.
  • Max Downtime Event is the longest single downtime.
  • When the selection has no work orders, a "No downtime records found" message takes the place of the cards.

An average far above the median means a few long events carry the total; an average close to the median means many similar events do.

The four views

  • Monthly Downtime Trend: downtime added up by the month of the work order date (UTC), with every month from the first to the last shown, quiet months at 0. Undated failures are left out of this chart only.
  • Downtime Distribution: how many work orders fall in each range: 0 to 1 hour, 1 to 4, 4 to 8, 8 to 24, and 24 hours or more. A value on a boundary goes in the higher range, so exactly 4 hours counts in 4 to 8.
  • Downtime by Asset / Location: downtime added up by asset name, or by asset number where no name is recorded, highest first. Work orders do not record a location, so the Top Downtime Events table shows the root or parent asset in the hierarchy in its Location column instead.
  • Top Downtime Events: the ten failures with the most downtime; ties go to the most recent.

What it reads

  • PMs: every completed PM on the asset, whatever it targets. A record is a PM when its saved class is PM or PM reset candidate, or, while no class has been saved, when the automatic classifier flagged it as a PM. It is completed when it has a completion date, or is marked complete and its status does not read "incomplete" or "not complete". Its date is equation C1.
  • Failures: the included failures of the selected mechanism, matched within the selected failure mode when one is given.
  • Undated records: a PM or failure without a usable date is left out, because it cannot be paired.

Check the PMs you rely on

Until a record has a saved class, the automatic flag decides whether it is a PM, and that flag can mistake a return-to-service note such as "RTS at 4:30 PM" for one. Saving a disposition settles it only with the right Record Class: that column starts at the automatic class, PM for a record like this, so change it to Corrective WO before saving.

Pairing PMs with failures

Each PM is paired with the first failure strictly after it. A PM with no later failure is not paired. Several PMs with no failure between them all pair with the same next failure.

tp,tf
The dates of PM p and failure f, to the second, so days to failure can be fractional.
P∗
The PMs that were paired with a failure.
