Analysis

Perform an Analysis offers five analysis types, each run on one asset at a time. All five start from the dispositions people save, on the Disposition page or from a record's link in an analysis table, except that PM effectiveness picks its PMs by their record class. The first card covers what every analysis reads; the other five explain each analysis in depth.

Dispositions decide what counts

No analysis reads a work order's text to decide whether it was a failure. An editor dispositions each work order once, on the Disposition page or from a task ID in any analysis table, under the work-order disposition standard: a category, then where it failed (sub-asset) and what (component), how (failure mechanism, mode and cause, in the ISO 14224 lists), what was done (action) and how sure the record is (evidence). Saving again replaces the current disposition; earlier ones are kept as history, but no analysis reads them.

On the Disposition page the asset's work orders are listed on the left; the ▾ on each column header sorts the list by that column or keeps only the values ticked, across every page of it, and Download Excel takes the rows the list shows, in its order. The work order picked opens on the right with each part of it under its own label: its created, started and completed times on the plant's clock, its downtime and record class, the request title and description, the failure narrative (area affected, condition found, cause and action taken) and the completion notes. A part the record left empty says so. A work order filed under 4002 can carry the name of the sub-asset it was on; the card then shows that name too, as Asset named on the work order. The sub-asset dropdown starts at the asset itself ("4002 (whole asset)") and offers every asset under it, at every level, picked as the PM calendar picks them: by Limble's hierarchy, including sub-assets with no work orders yet. An asset Limble's list does not have follows the plant's numbering (4002-S01 to 4002-S18 under 4002). A line under the dropdown says which it read.

The analysis population

Every analysis on Perform an Analysis reads one population. Picking an asset puts in reach its own work orders, those of its sub-assets, and those dispositioned to a sub-asset under it. The selector then narrows it:

  • Sub-asset and component choose which items' lives are pooled in the Weibull, and which work orders the pattern views count. The sub-asset list shows the asset itself, then each of its sub-assets under its parent; a Pareto bar or matrix cell narrows to one as well.
  • Failure mechanism, mode, cause and action choose which failures count. In the Weibull, a failure of the pooled items that does not match is a competing failure: it ends the life as a suspension when it renewed the item.
  • Disposition categories narrow the pattern views only (Failure, unless more are ticked); the Weibull has its own rule for each category.
  • Induced failures (installation or maintenance errors) can be counted as suspensions instead.
  • From and To narrow every view by date, and are a Weibull run's start and cutoff.

Leaving a list unticked puts no restriction on it. Clicking a Pareto bar or a matrix cell narrows the population to it and steps down a level (sub-asset, then component, then mechanism); each narrowing shows as a chip that takes it back out. The population is also written as one line, its definition, which every saved Weibull run keeps, for example POP: asset=4002; location=4002-S09; component=Filter; failure_mech=CONT; suspensions=other renewals; window=2024-01-02..2025-09-30.

Why this can differ from availability

The Availability card charges downtime to the month a work order was opened. The pattern views date it by when the event happened, so the two usually agree, but a work order with no created date can sit in different months on the Metrics page and here.

What it answers

Weibull analysis describes how long one failure mode or mechanism lets one asset run between failures. Its two parameters answer two questions. β, the shape, says whether failures come early in life, at random, or from wear-out. η, the scale, says how long a typical life is. Together they give the chance of failing by any age, the mean life, and the ages by which 10% and 50% are expected to fail.

It works from lives rather than from counts of failures. Every stretch of running between two events on the asset is one life. A life that ended in a failure says when this failure arrived; one that ended any other way says only that it had not arrived yet. The sections below follow those lives in order: how they are cut from the work order history, what shape the Weibull distribution gives them, how β and η are fitted to them, and how to read the charts and the summary GREMLIN draws from the fit.

Choosing the population

A Weibull fit only means something for one kind of item failing in one way, so it is run on the analysis population: its sub-assets and components choose the renewal streams pooled (one physical item each, such as the filter on 4002-S09), and its failure mechanism, mode, cause and action choose which of their failures count. Narrow it to one component, and a mechanism, before fitting; the result warns when it pools several components or mechanisms, is keyed on a failure mode, has few failures, rests on unknown mechanisms or induced failures, or leaves work orders out for want of a component.

  • A fit needs at least five lives that end in a failure, the minimum REL-WBL-MTH-001 §4 sets (REL-WBL-REQ-001 VV-071). With fewer, the lives are still listed but no fit is made; a fit on five is made and flagged as a small sample.
  • An unknown mechanism is read both ways. When a mechanism is selected and a failure with mechanism UNK could be it, the fit is made with the unknowns as suspensions (shown first) and again with them as failures, and both are shown.
  • The latest saved fit of exactly the population on screen opens by itself. Perform Analysis (editors) fits and saves a new run with its population definition; Preview the fit (any account) fits without saving.

Events, life intervals and censoring

Lives are built stream by stream, then pooled. A stream's events are the Failures of its component on its sub-asset, and the work that renewed it alongside: a PM reset listing it among its renewed items, or a failure of another item that renewed it too. In date order:

  • The first renewal only starts the clock. Nothing is known about the life before it, so no interval ends there.
  • A failure that counts for the population ends a complete life, δ=1, dated when it was found. When its action renewed the item (replace, repair, clean), the next life starts when the repair was finished; when it did not (reset, adjust, inspect), no life starts until the item is next renewed, and the stream is flagged as recurrent.
  • A failure of another kind that renewed the item ends the life as a suspension, δ=0: a competing failure reset the clock before this one had its chance.
  • A PM reset ends the running life as a suspension when the PM was finished, and starts the next one there.
  • The life still running at the analysis cutoff is the current life, right-censored too.
  • A failure dated before the repair that started the running life was finished makes no life and is noted as a possible duplicate. A life with no scheduled hours in it is left out.
  • A life that ends within an hour, in calendar time, of the event before it is flagged Check for duplicate in the data table's Check column: the near-zero life two work orders for one breakdown make pulls β below 1 (REL-WBL-DAT-004 §12).

Every event that touched a pooled stream gets a row in the event processing table under the data table, saying what it did there (starts a life, failure, suspension, no effect, left out) and why; an event with no date, or outside the analysis window, is listed and left out.

The analysis window

The lives are built between an optional start date and the analysis cutoff, and both are dates on the plant's calendar:

  • Start date: blank means all history. A date counts from that day's first minute, and the first event on or after it starts the clock.
  • Cutoff: a date runs to the end of that day, or to the moment of the run if that day is today, so an event at the midnight that ends the day belongs to the next one and is left out. Blank means the last completed Limble import, as long as that is later than every event, and otherwise the moment of the run. Censoring at the moment of the run would credit the current life with hours the database has heard nothing about yet, and a failure in them would be missed.
  • A completed date with no time, as an exported or hand-entered date can be, names a day on the plant's calendar, so the fit takes it as that day's first minute on the plant's clock. Read as midnight UTC it would be the evening before, and could fall on the wrong side of a start or cutoff date. Repeat Fix Rate reads it the same way.
  • They come from the population's From and To dates. A To date of today or later is left blank on purpose: a cutoff of today would censor the current life at the moment of the run, the very hours a blank cutoff exists to keep out, while a blank one ends at the last completed import.
  • The result states its window and where the cutoff came from: the last completed Limble import, the cutoff date entered for the run, or the time of the run.

Schedule-adjusted life hours

Life is counted in scheduled hours, not calendar hours, so an asset is not aged while it is not meant to run. Each interval is cut at every midnight on the plant's clock. On a weekday schedule a Saturday or Sunday adds nothing, and a weekday adds its elapsed hours scaled by the asset's scheduled hours per day:

{% call std.equation("W1", "a life in hours is the sum over the interval's weekday segments of the segment's elapsed hours times scheduled hours per day over 24") %} ti= ∑s∈weekdays of intervali Δs×h24 {% endcall %}
Δs
Elapsed hours of the interval that fall in day s, at most 24: 23 or 25 on the two days a year the clocks change, because each day's hours are measured between real instants.
h
Scheduled hours per day on the asset's Weibull schedule: 20 on the plant default, 20 hours Monday-Friday, and 24 on 24 hours Monday-Friday.

Every asset is on the plant default unless the Weibull schedules register on the Configuration page lists it (REL-WBL-DAT-003 §7). The register started with the Salvagnini assets 3101 to 3107 and the Hedrich dispensers 3154, 3142, 3023 and 3253 on 24 hours Monday-Friday. Its third schedule, continuous, is for an asset that runs around the clock: every hour counts, weekends included, so its life is the plain elapsed time. Editors change an asset's schedule there with a reason, and every change is kept with who made it and when. A saved result counted on the asset's old schedule then says so, is marked in the rankings and can't be reported until it is run again.

{% call std.figure("W2", "A life from Monday 08:00 to the next Monday 08:00 is 168 calendar hours. The 48 weekend hours are left out, and the 120 weekday hours count at 20/24, giving 100 life hours for an asset on the 20-hour schedule.") %} {% endcall %}

Days are split at midnight in the plant's time zone, the one the Availability card judges months by (America/Chicago unless it has been changed), so the weekend left out is the plant's own Saturday and Sunday rather than UTC's, which runs from Friday evening to Sunday evening on the plant's clock. If that zone can't be loaded, days are split at midnight UTC and the result carries a notice saying so. The dates themselves are still stored in UTC.

Life hours are scheduled hours, so a calendar week holds five scheduled days of h: 100 life hours on the 20-hour schedule and 120 on the 24-hour one, and 168 on the continuous one. That is how the page and the report turn η, MTTF, B10 and B50 into calendar weeks.

The Weibull distribution

Lives of one failure behavior are never all the same length: some parts fail early, most somewhere in the middle, a few run long. The Weibull distribution is a family of shapes that spread can take, and two numbers pick one member of the family. With shape β and scale η, the chance a life lasts beyond age t (its reliability), the chance it has ended by then, the density of failing at t, and the instantaneous failure rate of a survivor are:

{% call std.equation("W2", "reliability is e to the minus t over eta to the power beta") %} R(t)= exp[−(tη)β] {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W3", "unreliability, the cumulative distribution, is one minus reliability") %} F(t)=1−R(t) {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W4", "the probability density is beta over eta times t over eta to the power beta minus one times reliability") %} f(t)= βη (tη)β−1 R(t) {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("W5", "the hazard rate is the density over reliability, which is beta over eta times t over eta to the power beta minus one") %} h(t)= f(t)R(t)= βη (tη)β−1 {% endcall %}

The four answer different questions about the same lives, and each has its own place on Perform an Analysis:

At t=η, whatever β is, F(η)=1−e−1≈0.632: η is the age by which 63.2% of the population is expected to have failed. η only sets the scale: double it and every age doubles, η, B10, B50 and MTTF with it, while each curve keeps its shape. β sets the shape itself, and above all how the hazard moves with age:

  • β below 1: the hazard falls. Parts that are going to fail tend to do it early, from a defect, a poor installation or a poor repair, and the ones that come through are the sound ones. A part that has run a while is safer than a new one.
  • β of 1: the hazard is level, at 1/η per hour: the exponential distribution. Age tells you nothing. A part that has run 1,000 hours is exactly as likely to fail in the next hour as a new one, because its failures come from outside events rather than from use: a jam, a dropped load, a power spike.
  • β between 1 and 2: the hazard rises, more slowly with every hour. Wear is setting in, but lives are still widely spread.
  • β of 2: the hazard climbs in a straight line with age.
  • β around 3.5: the density is close to symmetric, much like a bell curve, with lives gathered a little below η.
  • β well above 3: steep wear-out. Lives bunch tightly just below η, and the risk climbs fast once B10 is passed.
{% call std.figure("W3", "Hazard rate against age, in multiples of η, for three values of β. Below 1 the risk of failing falls with age, at 1 it stays level, and above 1 it climbs: the three patterns the interpretation rules below are built on.") %} {% endcall %}

Over a whole working life the three patterns make the bathtub curve: a falling hazard while early defects work themselves out, a level stretch of random failures, then a rise as wear sets in. One fit describes one failure behavior, so it sees whichever part of the bathtub that behavior sits in. That is one more reason a population is one mechanism, or one mode, at a time.

Why β decides whether an age-based PM can help

A PM or replacement on a schedule swaps a part that has run for one that has not. That lowers the chance of a failure only if the fresh part is less likely to fail soon than the one it replaces, and the hazard says which is. With β above 1 the old part is the riskier one, so renewing it helps, and the higher β, the more. With β of 1 the two are equally risky: a scheduled replacement spends the labor and the part and leaves the failure rate where it was. With β below 1 the fresh part is the riskier one, and every renewal starts the risky early hours again. Equation C5 puts a number on it: the chance that a part already t hours old fails within the next Δ hours. Figure W4 sets that beside a new part's chance at any age.

Fitting β and η by maximum likelihood

Every pair of β and η is a different guess at how this mechanism's lives are spread. The fit scores each guess by how likely it makes the lives that actually happened, and keeps the guess that scores highest. Each life brings its own piece of evidence:

  • A complete life of ti hours says a failure arrived at that age. It scores the density there, f(ti) from equation W4: high when the guess puts many failures around that age, low when it says failures there are rare.
  • A censored life says only that the part ran at least that long without this failure. It scores the reliability, R(ti) from equation W2: the chance, under the guess, of lasting that long.

The likelihood is the product of every life's score, so a guess has to suit all the lives at once: one that crowds failures too early is marked down by the long lives, one that spreads them too late by the short ones. Multiplying many small numbers is awkward, so the fit adds their logarithms instead, which peak at the same β and η. With n lives of which r are complete:

{% call std.equation("W8", "one over beta, plus the mean log of the complete lives, minus the t to the beta weighted mean log of all lives, equals zero") %} 1β+ 1r∑i:δi=1ln⁡ti − ∑i=1ntiβln⁡ti ∑i=1ntiβ =0 {% endcall %}
  • How it is solved: GREMLIN checks 400 evenly spaced values of β from 0.1 to 20 for a change of sign in equation W8, then halves the bracketing interval 80 times. η then follows from equation W7.
  • If there is no root between 0.1 and 20, the fit stops and says it did not converge. There is then no maximum-likelihood β to report, and REL-WBL-MTH-001 §5.5 makes β and η maximum-likelihood estimates, so no estimate of another kind is put in its place. It happens when the lives are all but identical, often because of duplicate work orders.
  • The fit needs at least five complete lives, lives that end in a failure. With fewer, or with no root, the run stops and says why. Over all of the group's history, any result saved for it before is then removed, because the data no longer supports it. Over a window an editor entered, the saved result is kept: a window too narrow to fit says nothing against the whole history.
Why censored lives have to count

It is tempting to fit the failures alone, or to treat every life as if it ended in a failure. Both make a mechanism look shorter-lived than it is. Fitted three ways, the worked example's lives give:

Confidence intervals

The approximate 95% intervals come from how sharply the log-likelihood falls away from its peak. GREMLIN takes its second derivatives numerically with respect to ln⁡β and ln⁡η, inverts that information matrix for the variances, and turns them back, so both ends of each interval are always positive:

{% call std.equation("W9", "the interval for beta is beta times e to the minus or plus 1.96 standard errors of log beta, and the same for eta, with the squared standard errors read off the inverse of the observed information") %} βL,U=β^exp(∓1.96SE(ln⁡β^)) ηL,U=η^exp(∓1.96SE(ln⁡η^)) SE2(ln⁡β^)=[ℐ−1]11 SE2(ln⁡η^)=[ℐ−1]22 {% endcall %}
ℐ
The observed information: the negative of the matrix of second derivatives of equation W6 with respect to ln⁡β and ln⁡η, taken at the fitted values. GREMLIN estimates it by finite differences.
1.96
The two-sided 95% point of the standard normal distribution.

The lower pane of figure W5 shows where the width comes from. A sharp peak in the profile log-likelihood means only values of β close to the best explain the lives nearly as well, so the interval is narrow; a flat top means a wide range explains them almost as well, so it is wide. More complete lives sharpen the peak, and the interval narrows roughly with the square root of their number: halving its width takes about four times the failures. The worked example's five failures leave β's interval 154% as wide as β itself; the lab's twenty-failure wear-out preset brings that down to 65%, inside the 70% the interpretation summary calls stable.

When the curvature cannot be inverted the intervals are reported as Not available. They are approximate either way: with few failures they are wide, and intervals of this kind also tend to come out narrower than they should when failures are few, so read a narrow one on a small sample with care.

On the charts: B10, B50 and η are where the CDF crosses 10%, 50% and 63.2%, read down to the age axis, and MTTF is the balance point of the PDF; figure W6 marks all four. How to read it:

  • The slope is β. A steep line is a high β: failures bunched in a narrow band of ages, wear-out. A shallow one is a low β: failures spread from very early to very late. The panes are not drawn to equal scales, so read β from the result rather than from the angle.
  • Where the line crosses 63.2%, which is ln(−ln R) = 0, is η.
  • The points against the line are the check on the model. Points scattered on both sides of one straight line support one Weibull population. The first few wander most, because each is one failure among few lives.
  • The two yellow lines are the line at the lower ends of both intervals and at the upper ends: how far it could swing and slide on this much data. With few failures they splay wide apart.
  • A point the plot cannot draw, with an estimate of 0 or 1, such as the last failure when no life outlasts it, is left off.
  • On Perform an Analysis the axes are labelled ln(life hours) and ln(−ln R), with no tick values; the figures here label them in hours and in the share failed.
How straight is straight enough: R²

How straight the points lie is measured by the probability plot's R²: the squared correlation of the plotted points, which is also the R² of the least-squares line through them (REL-WBL-REQ-001 VV-070). It is 1 when they sit exactly on a line. It checks the model, not the fit: β and η come from equation W8, not from a line through these points, so adjusting them leaves R² where it is.

{% call std.equation("W15", "R squared is the squared sum of products of the plotted points' deviations from their means, over the product of the sums of their squared deviations") %} R2= [∑j(xj−x¯)(yj−y¯)]2 ∑j(xj−x¯)2∑j(yj−y¯)2 {% endcall %}
xj,yj
The points on the probability plot, equation W14 at each age where a complete life ends. A point the plot cannot draw, with an estimate of 0, is left out here too.
x¯,y¯
Their averages. With fewer than three points R² is not shown, because two points always lie on a line.

Probability Density (PDF)

The PDF plots f(t), equation W4: how thickly failures fall at each age. The area under it between two ages is the share of lives that end in a failure between them, and the whole area is 1; its peak is the commonest age at failure. Its shape is β's signature:

  • β below 1: highest at age 0 and falling steeply, with a long tail of lives that run long.
  • β of 1: highest at 0 too, falling away exponentially.
  • β above 1: a hump, starting at 0, rising to a peak and falling again. The higher β, the narrower the hump and the closer its peak to η.

A narrow hump means lives are predictable: most fail within a narrow band of ages, which is what makes a fixed PM interval worth setting. A wide one means the age at failure varies a lot, so any fixed interval either renews many parts long before they would fail or lets many fail first. The PDF is drawn from the fit alone, with no points and no interval curves, at the fitted values or the values an editor is trying.

Hazard Rate

The Hazard Rate plots h(t), equation W5: for a part that has run t hours without failing, how fast failure is coming at it now. It is the chart that answers the PM question directly (see The Weibull distribution):

  • Falling, β below 1: the longer a part has run, the safer it is. Renewing it on a schedule restarts the risky early hours; removing the defects behind them is the better aim.
  • Level, β of 1: the same risk at every age, 1/η per hour. A schedule cannot lower it; inspection or condition monitoring that catches a failure as it starts can.
  • Rising, β above 1: the risk climbs with age. Renewing the part before it climbs too far lowers it, and where the curve starts to climb steeply is where an interval is worth weighing against downtime and cost.

Over a short span, the hazard times the span is close to the chance of failing in it: a hazard of 2 per 1,000 hours is about a 1% chance in the next 5 hours. Over a longer span the hazard changes as the part ages, so use equation C5, as the Most likely to fail soon list does. Like the PDF, the hazard is drawn from the fit alone. Its height depends on η as well as β, so compare its shape, not its scale, between fits.

Reading a result in five questions
  1. Do the points look like one population? R² against its threshold, and the shape of the probability plot (figure W8). If not, fix the population, by fitting a mechanism or reviewing a duplicate, before reading anything else.
  2. Which side of 1 is β, and does its interval stay there? That chooses the strategy: removing defects below 1, detection at 1, renewal by age above it.
  3. How wide are the intervals? Wide ones make the reading a direction rather than a decision. More failures, or a cleaner population, narrow them.
  4. Where does the current life sit? Against B10 and η on the CDF: a wear-out mechanism whose current life is past B10 is already where its hazard climbs.
  5. How likely is a failure in the next few weeks? Equation C5 puts the hazard and the current life together, and the Most likely to fail soon list ranks the asset's mechanisms by it.

Adjusting, saving and reporting

  • Each failure group keeps only its latest run. A run saves its dataset, lives, Kaplan-Meier points, curve and result, stamped with its analysis window, and viewers see that saved result. Running the group again picks up every disposition saved since and replaces the previous run, along with any adjusted parameters saved against it, so earlier runs are not kept. A run the data can no longer support, with fewer than five failure lives or no likelihood root, removes the group's saved result too. A failure mode and each mechanism under it are separate groups: running one leaves the others' saved results as they were.
  • Every run is stamped with the method it was built by (life-data-v3 today) and the GREMLIN code it ran on. A result saved under an earlier method shows a notice saying what has changed since; it stays in the rankings, marked, but can't be reported until it is run again. So does a result counted on a schedule its asset has since been moved off, and one whose days were split at midnight in a time zone other than the plant's current one: a change of the plant's zone, or the zone database being installed after a run fell back to UTC without it, moves every life's hours. One resting on fewer than five failure lives is not ranked or reported either.
  • Editors can try other values of β and η (the inputs step by 0.2 and by 100 hours) and the charts redraw as they type. Save Adjusted Parameters stores the pair with a reason beside the fitted result, never replacing it. A saved pair lasts until the group is run again, and the page does not show it again: the inputs always open on the fitted values.
  • Generate Weibull Report, for editors, writes a Word report numbered REL-WBL-RPT, the asset number and a running number. It carries what REL-WBL-MTH-001 §10 asks of an analysis package, at the fitted values: the population and its grouping level; the life basis, schedule and time zone, labelled as an exposure proxy; the analysis window and where its cutoff came from; the lives, split into complete, censored at a PM reset and current; β, η, MTTF, B10, B50, their intervals and the reliability at the age entered on the page; the charts; the interpretation summary; the limitations and assumptions; every life it rests on, with its notes; and the method and code version. It opens with REL-WBL-VAL-001's caveat that GREMLIN's Weibull automation is not yet validated, so the report is for reliability engineering review, not a stand-alone maintenance directive. A failure-mode report also asks for, states and keeps with the population the reason a mechanism could not be fitted instead (REL-WBL-PLN-003 §8). The report is downloaded rather than stored: GREMLIN logs its number, so that no number is used twice, but keeps no copy. Keep the download if a run's results need a record, because running the group again replaces them.
  • Clicking a Task ID in the Weibull data table opens that work order's disposition for an editor; saving it runs the analysis again, which replaces the saved run as above. Viewers see the Task ID as plain text.

Limitations

  • Few failures give wide intervals. Read β and η as directions until the intervals narrow.
  • A failure mode that pools different mechanisms mixes different behaviors and blurs β. Fit the mechanism when there are enough failures.
  • Life is scheduled time, not run time. It uses the asset's Weibull schedule, not meter hours: holidays and shutdowns are not taken out, and Saturday shifts on a weekday schedule are not added back.
  • No installation or replacement dates. The first event in the window only starts the clock, so the life before it is never counted.
  • Only two parameters. There is no failure-free period (three-parameter Weibull) and no comparison against other distributions.
  • Not yet validated. REL-WBL-VAL-001 validates the manual pilot workbook, not GREMLIN, and does not yet approve automated use (§7). Until GREMLIN's own results have been checked against the pilot baseline, use them for reliability engineering review rather than as stand-alone maintenance decisions.

{% endcall %} {# ====================================================================== Repeat fix rate ====================================================================== #} {% call std.doc_card( "analysis-repeat", "Repeat fix rate", "Counts how often a failure of the population comes straight back after a repair, item by item.", eyebrow="Repeat Fix Rate Analysis") %}

What it reads

The population's failures that have a component, taken item by item: each sub-asset and component is one stream, as in the Weibull. A failure with no date cannot be placed, and one dated after today can only be a data-entry error, so both are left out and the page says how many were; so are failures with no component, which belong to no item.

Each item's failures are put in the order they were found. A PM reset in between neither starts nor ends a gap: the question is whether the last repair held, not how long the part has run.

Gaps and repeats

The gap runs from when the previous failure's repair was finished to when the next failure of the same item was found, in scheduled hours, counted exactly as equation W1 counts a life: on the sub-asset's schedule in the Weibull schedule register (a sub-asset takes its parent's), the plant default otherwise, with days split at midnight on the plant's clock. A repair finished on Friday afternoon that fails again first thing on Monday has had only a few hours of running, and the weekend does not hide that.

{% call std.equation("R1", "a failure is a repeat when the scheduled hours since the last failure of the same mechanism are at most the repeat window, 24 hours unless another is entered") %} gj=L(tj−1,tj) repeat when gj≤w {% endcall %} {% call std.equation("R2", "the repeat fix rate is the number of repeats over the number of gaps, a mechanism with n failures having n minus one gaps") %} RFR=|{j:gj≤w}|n−1 {% endcall %}
tj
The completed date of the mechanism's j-th failure, j=2,…,n.
L
Scheduled life hours between two instants, equation W1.
w
The repeat window, in scheduled hours: 24 unless another is entered on the page, from more than 0 up to 720.

The asset's rate on the first summary card is the same ratio over every mechanism together: all the repeats over all the gaps.

{% call std.figure("R1", "A repair finished on Friday at 16:00 and the next failure of the same mechanism completed on Monday at 10:00. That is 66 calendar hours, but the weekend counts for nothing and the 8 Friday and 10 Monday hours count at 20/24, so on the 20-hour schedule the gap is 15 scheduled hours: within the 24-hour window, a repeat.") %} {% endcall %}

The four summary cards

  • Repeat rate: equation R2 over every item in the population, with the repeats and gaps behind it. With no item having two dated failures there is no gap, and it reads as a dash.
  • Highest rate: the item with the highest rate among those with at least five gaps and one repeat, so one quick repeat out of two failures cannot top it.
  • Most repeats: the item with the most repeats, whatever its number of gaps.
  • Possible duplicates are named under the cards: the repeats found less than an hour after the previous repair, or before it was even finished. Two work orders for one breakdown look exactly like that (REL-WBL-DAT-004 §12).

  • Mon 09:00 to Tue 08:0023 h × 20/24 = 19.2 scheduled hours: a repeat
  • Tue 08:00 to Fri 16:00 the next week200 weekday hours × 20/24 = 166.7 scheduled hours: the fix held
  • Fri 16:00 to Mon 10:00, figure R115 scheduled hours: a repeat
  • Mon 10:00 to Mon 10:00 two weeks on200 scheduled hours: the fix held

Tables and the window

  • Repeat fix rate by item lists every item with its schedule, failures, gaps, repeats and rate, the most repeats first. The line above it names the schedules and time zone the hours were counted on.
  • Repeat failures lists each repeat with the fix before it: both task IDs, when the fix was finished and the repeat found, their mechanisms, and the gap in scheduled and in calendar hours. Editors can click either task ID to review its disposition. Clicking a row in the table above lists only that item's repeats; clicking it again, or Show every item, lists them all.
  • The window box changes w and counts again.

Reading it: a high rate says repairs on that mechanism often do not hold: a wrong diagnosis, a part fitted or adjusted badly, or the work order closed before the fault was cleared. It is also one reason a Weibull fit of the same mechanism can come out with beta below 1, since each quick repeat is a very short life. The analysis is report-only: the Weibull fit keeps every one of those lives, and nothing here changes it. Check the possible duplicates first, and read the repeats' narratives before deciding the repair, not the PM, is what needs changing.
