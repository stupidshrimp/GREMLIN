How GREMLIN Works

GREMLIN reads maintenance records from Limble, lets people classify them, and turns them into metrics and analyses. This tab explains how the pieces fit together, from the data underneath to the pages, accounts and tools on top.

{% call std.figure("S1", "How records move through GREMLIN. Limble is the system of record; GREMLIN keeps a copy, adds the decisions people make about each record, and calculates the metrics and analyses from those every time a page asks, apart from Weibull fits, which are saved when an editor runs one.") %} {% endcall %}
{% call std.doc_card( "site-data", "From Limble to GREMLIN", "Where the records come from, how often they are refreshed, and what happens to them on the way in.", eyebrow="Data") %}
  • Limble is the system of record. GREMLIN reads from Limble and never writes back to it, so most corrections are made in Limble and flow in with the next sync. Three kinds do not: a work order moved to another asset stays under the asset it was first stored under, a field cleared in Limble keeps its old value here (dates and the four instruction boxes excepted), and a task deleted in Limble stays in GREMLIN.
  • The Limble sync runs every night as a scheduled job. An administrator can also start it from the Developer area when a correction has to show up sooner. Each run pulls every task (work orders and PMs) and every asset. It also reads the Area Affected, Condition, Cause and Action boxes from the instructions of completed tasks it has not read yet or that changed since, up to 500 tasks per run by default, newest first, working back through the history.
  • Raw records keep every field Limble sent for each task, with GREMLIN's own additions stored alongside (the asset number, its place in the hierarchy, readable UTC dates and the four instruction boxes), so the mapping can always be redone. Template tasks, which are PM and work order definitions rather than events, are left out.
  • Mapped records copy the fields GREMLIN works with into columns of their own: the asset number (Limble's asset ID, or the number older records used for the same asset), the asset's name and place in the asset hierarchy, the created, start and completed dates (stored in UTC), status, Limble's task type, downtime converted from seconds to hours, the description and completion notes, and the Area Affected, Condition, Cause and Action boxes.
  • The mapping also guesses a record class for every record; the next card explains how, and why a person's call replaces it.
  • The mapping is rebuilt by itself whenever its rules change, and editors can catch it up from Configuration with Refresh CMMS mapping, which maps any records imported or changed since the last mapping.
  • GREMLIN keeps its data in four SQLite files, so a backup of GREMLIN.db alone is not enough:
    • GREMLIN.db: the Limble records, dispositions, failure modes and mechanisms, Weibull results, and the availability configuration, goals and overtime.
    • accesscontrol.db, beside GREMLIN.db: the accounts and their login and change history.
    • auxillary.db, in the GREMLIN Global DB folder on the shared drive: the bug reports, one list for every installation.
    • PM_Calendar_local.db, beside GREMLIN.db: the PM Task Tracker's copy of PM tasks. It holds only what it pulls from Limble, and every pull fetches all of it again.
    Back up the first three. Those are their default places; an installation can move any of the four, and the Developer area shows where the first three are.

The automatic record class

As each record is mapped, GREMLIN reads its Limble type, its name, its request title and description, its completion notes and its tags, and takes the first of these rules that matches:

  1. Parts order: the text talks about ordering, delivering or stocking parts.
  2. Project work: the text mentions a project, an upgrade or an install.
  3. PM: Limble type 1, a PM code such as "- M -" or "- Q -" in the name, or the word "pm" in the text.
  4. Corrective work order: Limble type 6 (a work request), or repair words such as leaking, broken, fault, failed or replace.
  5. Inspection: inspection words with no repair words.
  6. Unknown: none of the above.

It is only a starting guess

The word "pm" also matches the clock in return-to-service notes such as "RTS at 4:30 PM", so real breakdowns can be guessed as PMs. That is why availability's hours and its Exclude PMs switch ignore the guess, which only names the non-PM segments of its stacked bars, and why a class a person saves on the Disposition page replaces it in every metric and analysis. The Disposition lists and the readiness counts still include any record the classifier flagged as a work order or PM.

Dispositioning

Editors open Disposition, pick an asset, choose Work Orders or PMs, and can narrow the list to the records that still need a decision. Each row takes a category, a failure mode and mechanism for a work order or a reset decision and target for a PM, the Include in Weibull Candidate flag, and notes. Saving keeps the previous decision as history. The same rows can be exported to Excel, filled in there and imported back.

Failure modes and mechanisms belong to each asset: typing a new name adds it to that asset's list when the row is saved, so later rows can reuse it and stay in the same population. What each category means for the analyses is on the Analysis tab, in What every analysis reads.

  • Signing in: use the person icon at the bottom of the sidebar, with a username and PIN. The login lasts for the browser session; closing the browser signs you out. Accounts are handed out by the people listed under Help.
  • Signed out, Home, Reliability Links, Standards and Documentation, and Configuration are open. Every other sidebar entry is still shown, struck through, and asks you to log in.
  • Viewer: opens every page their department is offered except Disposition, which is for editors. Configuration shows a viewer no settings, and Perform an Analysis shows a viewer the Weibull fit an editor last saved.
  • Editor: can also change stored data: dispositions, goals, overtime and configuration, running and adjusting Weibull analyses, and generating reports.
  • Administrator: can also open the Developer area.
  • Your department decides which pages the sidebar offers; each account also records a staff level, but no page is narrowed by it yet. Life Data Analysis, Metrics and Configuration are kept out of Operations & Maintenance accounts (Operations, Maintenance, or both), the Safety Report and Overdue WO Tracker are for every department except Facilities, and the PM Task Tracker is for every department. A page your department is not offered is left out of your sidebar and refused at its address.
  • Every change a signed-in account saves is recorded with who made it and when.

  • Show me around: guided tours on Home, Perform an Analysis, Disposition, Metrics and the PM Task Tracker, plus a walk-through of the Availability card.

{% endcall %} {% call std.doc_card( "site-changes", "When the numbers change", "What updates a number, and when.", eyebrow="Freshness") %}
  • New Limble records, and most corrections, reach GREMLIN.db with the nightly sync, or sooner if an administrator runs one; From Limble to GREMLIN lists the corrections that do not carry over. The PM Task Tracker keeps its own copy, refreshed when an editor presses Sync from Limble on that page.
  • Availability is recalculated every time the Metrics page loads, from the configuration as it is at that moment. See Asset availability by month.
  • The trend, downtime driver and PM effectiveness analyses read the current dispositions every time they load.
  • Weibull results are saved snapshots, one per failure group. Run the analysis again to include dispositions saved since; the new run replaces the group's previous one and any adjusted parameters saved with it. Changing a disposition from the Weibull data table re-runs it straight away.
  • Configuration changes to schedules, group membership and linked downtime recalculate every month of availability. Goals and overtime change one month at a time.
